Wattsense: Design and Reproducible Simulation of a
 Runtime-Triggered Priority Load Controller
 Dhanesh Chaudhari, Ujjwal Kshirsagar, Mrudula Pagar, Yash Ahire
 IEEE-style working draft | 30 September 2026 | Simulation study; author review pending
Abstract - This paper presents the design and reproducible
simulation of Wattsense, a low-power battery monitoring
and priority load controller. A four-layer interface board
connects an ESP32, current monitors, an external
converter, an essential lamp and a switchable fan to a
separately protected battery pack. A charge-counting
estimator supplies a runtime heuristic that triggers
optional-load shedding. The evaluation combines a lumped
discharge model, resistance extracted from selected PCB
paths, reduced SPICE circuits and software verification. In
an illustrative 6 Ah discharge from 68% to 20% state of
charge, with 2.5 W per load, 0.8 W controller demand and
90% converter efficiency, lamp service increases from
5.476 to 7.884 h while fan service decreases from 5.476 to
2.494 h. The corresponding changes are +43.96% and
-54.47%; they describe a service tradeoff, not an
equal-service efficiency gain. Eighteen deterministic
parameter pairs and six separate startup screening cases
expose dependence on assumed capacity, efficiency and
source limitations. Three startup cases fail a provisional
common-rail screen. The work provides an inspectable
implementation and identifies measurements needed for a
prototype. No physical measurements or solar-harvesting
performance are reported.
Index Terms - Battery monitoring, coulomb counting, priority
load control, embedded systems, printed circuit board,
reproducible simulation.
 I. Introduction
When a battery supplies loads with different priorities,
continued operation of every load can shorten the service
available to an essential function. Prior DC microgrid studies
have addressed this allocation problem using state-of-charge
thresholds and priority groups [1], as well as forecast-assisted
scheduling [2]. A small student-built system offers a narrower
design question: what service tradeoff follows from one
explicit shedding rule, and which electrical constraints must be
checked before constructing it?
Wattsense considers a 5 V lamp and fan supplied from a
nominal 12.8 V battery system through an external converter.
The lamp is essential; the fan is optional. Its local controller
estimates charge and requests fan disconnection when a
remaining-runtime heuristic stays below a configurable
threshold. A purchased battery protection system remains
responsible for cell-level protection. The proposed interface
includes a charging measurement channel, but the principal
comparison has zero charging current and does not evaluate
solar generation.
The contribution is an implementation case study connecting a
routed PCB, explicit controller logic and reproducible
numerical checks. The evaluation reports both loads' service,
the assumptions behind the battery model, and unsuccessful
startup screening cases. It does not propose a new
charge-estimation algorithm or an optimal scheduler. The
board has not been fabricated or measured for this study; all
performance outcomes below are synthetic.
 II. Related Work and Study Scope
Coulomb counting integrates current relative to an initial
charge reference. Movassagh et al. analyze errors associated
with current measurement, integration, capacity uncertainty
and timing [3]. Their analysis motivates explicit capacity and
initialization assumptions here. Agreement between a
simulator's true and estimated charge cannot establish sensor
accuracy when both use the same current and capacity.
Marahatta et al. describe priority-based power management for
a stand-alone DC microgrid with essential and nonessential
loads, battery state-of-charge decisions, monitoring and
experimental results [1]. Rajbhandari et al. address
essential-load scheduling with forecast and optimization
methods [2]. These studies establish that prioritization is
existing practice. Wattsense uses a single optional branch and
a transparent runtime trigger, without forecasts or an
optimization claim; no common-dataset comparison with those
controllers is attempted.
Zhang et al. estimate battery equivalent-circuit parameters and
state of charge, with lithium-ion cell measurements [4]. Their
work illustrates the need to characterize a battery model before
claiming predictive accuracy. Wattsense instead uses an
assumed voltage curve and resistance; its estimator
approximates energy using constant nominal voltage. The
resulting runtime is a no-future-charge heuristic, not a
guaranteed lower bound. The present evidence supports design
verification and model-based screening, with physical
validation left to a later study.
 III. Hardware and Control Architecture
Figure 1 shows the proposed power and information paths. A
protected four-series-cell LiFePO4 pack connects through an
external fuse/disconnect and the board's battery path. U1
measures signed battery current, positive during discharge; U2
measures converter-input load power. A third INA219 channel
is provisioned for charge-current measurement. The selected
INA219 supports bus and shunt-voltage measurements [5].
The external charger must match the pack; raw panel voltage is
not connected to the charger interface.

The 100 mm by 90 mm board uses four copper layers, with
two inner ground planes. Its nominal stackup is 1.6 mm, with 1
oz outer and 0.5 oz inner copper. Three 0.1 ohm shunts, branch
fuses, connectors, a fan MOSFET and flyback diode provide
the interface around purchased pack and converter modules.
The declared design limits are 2 A on the battery path and 1.5
A continuous on the 5 V path, including controller demand.
These are design limits pending qualification, not measured
current ratings. Four layers alone do not establish thermal or
electromagnetic performance.
An AO3400A switches the fan return; the lamp remains
connected. The startup model uses 48 milliohms for MOSFET
conduction, consistent with the selected part's
room-temperature maximum at 2.5 V gate drive [6]. Fuse
cold-resistance inputs are nominal data-sheet values [7], not
predictions of hot resistance or clearing time. A DS18B20 is
intended to monitor pack-surface temperature. The ESP32
makes local decisions and exports telemetry to a read-only
dashboard. The switch state in telemetry is a command, not an
independent measurement of load interruption.
A. Charge and runtime estimates
Let s be estimated state of charge in percent, C the configured
capacity in Ah before reserving charge, I the signed battery
current in A and dt the elapsed time in seconds. The
implemented update is
 s[k+1] = clip(s[k] - 100 Ieff dt / (3600 C), 0, 100). (1)
Here Ieff equals I during discharge and 0.98 I during charging.
The nominal voltage Vnom is 12.8 V and reserve sres is 20%.
Estimated energy above reserve and the load-based trigger
time are
 Eest = C Vnom max(s - sres, 0) / 100, (2)
 Test = Eest / PU2, PU2 > 0.2 W. (3)
Eest is in Wh and Test in hours. PU2 is measured upstream of the
load shunt and converter, so it includes downstream
conversion and distribution losses. It excludes battery/internal
and some upstream losses. Equation (2) also substitutes
nominal voltage for an integrated voltage curve. Consequently,
excluding future charging does not make (3) a
minimum-runtime guarantee. A separate net-discharge display
uses battery terminal power with a 3 s exponential filter; that
filtered denominator is not the denominator of (3).
B. Decision sequence and invalid data
Figure 2 summarizes the local policy. With the hardware
armed and measurements valid, three consecutive seconds
below Tmin = 3 h command the fan off and latch the decision.
Restoration requires an explicit request and a prospective
estimate of at least Tmin + 0.25 h. The reference firmware
configuration reserves a provisional 3.5 W increment for that
check; this must be replaced by measured optional-load input
power. The principal discharge comparison starts with the fan
on and does not exercise restoration.

Selected explicit front-copper paths are extracted from the
routed board. Their resistance is summed as rho L/(w t), using
rho = 1.724 x 10-8 ohm m and a temperature coefficient of
0.00393 per degree C. The extraction omits parallel plane/pour
paths, pad spreading, contact variation and inductive effects; it
is a trace-path approximation rather than a field solution. A
separate 25 um, 60 degrees C case changes assumed copper
parameters; it does not predict board temperature.
The steady/discharge model assigns constant delivered power
to each active load. For local supply V, series resistance R and
delivered power P, the low-current solution is
 I = 2P / [V + √(V2 - 4RP)]. (4)
An outer solution accounts for common output-path drop.
Converter input demand is output power divided by fixed
efficiency. The battery-side equation is solved similarly with
open-circuit voltage and total upstream series resistance. A
non-positive discriminant rejects the operating point. Charge is
integrated with a 1 s step; an additional 0.25 s run checks
discretization. Calculated chemical power equals delivered
load power plus converter and resistive losses within
numerical tolerance. The coupled discharge simulation
executes the Python reference controller. Production C++
control logic is tested separately on a host compiler; the plant
run is not firmware-in-the-loop or ESP32 execution.
B. Comparison and sensitivity scenarios
The baseline retains both loads by disabling the runtime
threshold while preserving operating limits. Priority mode uses
a 3 h threshold. Both begin at the same exact 68% charge
reference and stop at the first modeled true-SOC reserve or
low-voltage endpoint. Main outputs are essential and optional
service hours and delivered energy. This comparison measures
allocation against retained-load operation, not superiority over
an optimized controller.
A deterministic full factorial contains 18 paired scenarios:
capacities of 4.8 and 6 Ah, efficiencies of 80%, 90% and 95%,
and battery/BMS resistances of 0.05, 0.10 and 0.20 ohm. Each
run gives the estimator the same capacity as the plant, with
exact current and initial charge. Thus the scenario ranges do
not quantify unknown-capacity or sensor-error robustness, and
are not confidence intervals. A separate earlier arithmetic
example combines 4.8 Ah true versus 6 Ah configured
capacity and 0.50 A true versus 0.52 A reported current for
four hours to illustrate accumulated estimation error.
C. Separate startup and circuit screens
Six ngspice startup cases use a separate reduced circuit. Its
instantaneous behavioral source is Isrc = max(0, min(Ilim, (5 -
Vreg)/0.04)), with volts, amperes and ohms. The output
capacitor is 470 uF with 0.05 ohm ESR, except one 2200 uF
case. A 100 ms resistive-equivalent fan demand produces
voltage-dependent current; controller current is 0.16 A. This
differs deliberately from the discharge model's constant-power
loads and fixed efficiency. It is not a converter
switching/control-loop simulation or a brushless-fan
electromechanical model.
The startup screen uses a provisional 4.75 V common-rail
floor, independent of any selected device's actual operating
limit. Other reduced circuits check shunt DC losses, fan
switching, gate discharge and I2C rise time. For the latter, a
first-order RC estimate tr approximately equals 0.8473 R C,
consistent with TI's pull-up calculation method [8]. Passing
these screens cannot establish reset immunity, successful fan
startup, fuse survival or electromagnetic compatibility.
D. Reproduction and verification
The local package includes Python models, raw CSV traces,
ngspice circuits, firmware sources, native KiCad files and an
independent evidence-check script. The main suite invokes
unit tests, native C++ policy/register checks, SPICE, system
scenarios and hardware-model scenarios. The publication
recheck also compares saved numerical summaries with raw
traces and independently integrates the nominal discharge
equations. KiCad 10.0.6 checks electrical rules, routing
connectivity and schematic parity. These activities verify
consistency of artifacts and equations; they are not physical
experiments.
 V. Results
A. Service allocation at the nominal point
Figure 3 and Table II report the nominal paired outcome. The
baseline provides 5.4764 h to each load. Priority mode
disconnects the fan at 2.4936 h and supplies the lamp until
7.8836 h. Lamp service gains 2.4072 h (+43.96%), while fan
service loses 2.9828 h (-54.47%). Both trajectories end at the
same true-charge reserve. This is a redistribution of service
under the stated assumptions.

B. DC operating point and parameter dependence
At the nominal initial operating point, total converter-output
current is 1.2077 A and battery current is 0.5196 A. The
common rail, lamp and fan terminals are 4.8847, 4.8342 and
4.7461 V, respectively. The fan's allowable supply range is
therefore a component-selection constraint even in steady
operation. Modelled converter loss is 0.6709 W, output-path
loss 0.2384 W and combined loss in the two active shunts
0.0540 W. These are dissipated-power calculations, not
temperature measurements.

Figure 5 includes every startup screen. Three of six cases
remain above the provisional common-rail floor. With a 1.8 A
equivalent fan startup demand and 1.5 A source limit, the
common rail falls to approximately 2.995 V. Increasing
capacitance from 470 to 2200 uF does not remove the eventual
sag during the sustained assumed demand. A 3 A source case
still reaches 4.694 V on the common rail because of
series-path losses; this scenario does not increase the board's
declared current limit.
For the smaller starter case, with 0.5 A nominal lamp/fan
demands, 1.0 A equivalent fan startup demand and 2 A source
limit, the common-rail minimum is 4.7907 V. The
fan-terminal minimum is only 4.5512 V. A rail-screen pass
therefore does not qualify a fan or prove processor reset
immunity. Actual converter current limiting, fan electronics,
wiring, pulse duration and fuse behavior must be
characterized.
The shunt DC screen gives 0.4 W in each 0.1 ohm shunt at 2
A; two series shunts then drop 0.4 V and dissipate 0.8 W. The
I2C RC screen gives approximately 746 ns with 2.2 kohm and
400 pF, compared with 1593 ns for 4.7 kohm and 400 pF. This
supports the chosen pull-up value for the modeled 100 kHz
bus load, while actual capacitance and waveform quality
remain unmeasured.
D. Artifact and numerical checks
The refreshed suite passes 45 Python unit tests and 67 native
C++ assertions, including locked/armed policy behavior and
injected register-read faults. The independent publication
recheck passed 38 checks before separately logged
documentation wording corrections. Fresh ERC/DRC reports
contain zero reported violations, zero unconnected items and
zero schematic-parity differences under the saved rule
configuration. Four ERC and five DRC categories are
configured as ignored and disclosed in the evidence package.
A scratch check enabling the ignored categories reports six
generic connector footprint-filter mismatches (J1-J6), while
independent pin, pitch and drill checks match the selected
terminals. The fabrication archive matches its audited files;
these facts do not constitute manufacturing or safety
qualification.
Changing the integration step from 1 s to 0.25 s changes
nominal baseline and priority service by 0.50 and 0.75 s,
respectively. Independent quadrature of the same equations
agrees with the saved service endpoints within one second.
The agreement checks numerical implementation of the
assumed model, not agreement with an actual battery or
converter.
 VI. Limitations and Hardware Validation Plan
The central limitation is absence of physical validation. The
OCV curve, pack resistance, wiring resistance, constant load
power and converter efficiency are assumed. The model omits
aging, temperature-dependent pack behavior, cell imbalance,
dynamic protection cutoffs, ADC noise and timing, converter
control dynamics and actual fan startup. Surface-temperature
limits and calculated dissipation do not establish thermal
safety. Solar generation, maximum-power-point tracking,
charge control and forecast-based scheduling are outside the
evaluated scope.
The capacity sweep is not a measurement-uncertainty analysis.
Its exact capacity, current and initialization deliberately favor
estimator agreement. The retained-load baseline also provides
no evidence of optimality. Fixed-SOC shedding, an
essential-only upper-service reference and threshold sweeps
would strengthen a later comparison. These additional
comparisons are proposed, not reported results.
A hardware validation sequence should first qualify the
purchased pack, matched charger, converter and selected
loads, and measure permitted terminal voltages and startup
demand. Bring-up should begin with a current-limited
laboratory supply and checked polarity, continuity and isolated
USB/power connections. Each current channel should be
calibrated against an independent instrument in the intended
operating range. Startup captures should record converter,
common-rail and fan-terminal voltages together with current,
followed by loaded temperature measurements.
For the service comparison, repeated paired baseline and
priority trials should use the same characterized pack, initial
charge reference, loads, environmental conditions and
independently tracked stopping criterion. Record both loads'
operating time, input/output energy, calibration uncertainty
and failure events. Repeated cycling and order effects should

 VII. Conclusion
Wattsense provides a traceable design and simulation case
study for runtime-triggered priority load control. Under the
illustrative nominal assumptions, lamp service increases by
43.96% while fan service decreases by 54.47%. Separate
startup screens reveal electrical constraints that a runtime-only
model would miss. Reproducible software, circuit and CAD
checks support implementation consistency. Physical
performance, component compatibility and any solar-system
benefit remain to be established through the stated
measurements.
